Joint Space vs. 6D vs. SE(3): What Action Representation Actually Does to a Policy

Part 1 of a series on the SAGE-VLA peg-transfer project. Experiments referenced here are from a surgical-robotics peg-transfer task (a 6-DOF PSM arm, PyBullet simulation, ACT and SmolVLA policies trained via imitation learning on scripted-oracle demonstrations).

It's tempting to treat the action space of a robot learning policy as a bookkeeping detail — twelve joint angles, six Cartesian numbers, whatever the robot happens to expose. Pick one, normalize it, train. The model is supposed to be the hard part; the representation is supposed to be plumbing.

It isn't. Across a few months of building out this project's imitation-learning stack, action representation turned out to be one of the highest-leverage decisions in the whole pipeline — not because the math of one representation is harder than another, but because each choice changes what's learnable, what's redundant, what's ambiguous, and in one case, what's flatly buggy. This post walks through joint-space, 6D rotation, and SE(3)/Cartesian action representations, using real findings (and one real regression) from this project as the evidence, not textbook argument.

The default nobody questions: joint space

Our arm is a 6-DOF serial mechanism (PSM: yaw, pitch, insertion, roll, wrist pitch, wrist yaw) plus a parallel-jaw gripper, plus four passive pitch-linkage joints that exist purely to realize the wrist mechanism in the URDF. Recorded naively, that's 12 numbers per tick: 6 arm joints, 2 gripper joints, 4 linkage joints.

Joint space is the path of least resistance for a reason: it's exactly what the simulator (or the real robot's low-level controller) already reports and consumes. No inverse kinematics needed at training time, no extra coordinate transforms, no ambiguity about which frame you're in. ACT's original paper uses joint space for bimanual manipulation, and our own ACT policy (the project's "stage 3") does too. It works — our joint-space ACT checkpoint hit 95.5% on its frozen benchmark.

But "it works" and "it's the right representation" are different claims, and three things this project actually ran into make the gap visible.

Cautionary tale #1: delta actions are not a free lunch

Before touching rotation representations at all, we tested the most common alternative encoding within joint space itself: predicting a per-tick delta (how far to move each joint) instead of an absolute target (where each joint should end up).

The result was unambiguous: 0% success vs. 27.5% for absolute actions, at matched data scale. Root-caused via offline replay: the model's predicted delta magnitude was essentially uncorrelated with the ground-truth delta magnitude (Pearson r = 0.08). It had learned a near-constant "medium" step size instead of modulating by state — good enough to approach a target, never able to decelerate into the millimeter-scale success radius the task demands.

The lesson generalizes past joint space: a delta representation asks a model to get both direction and magnitude right on every single step, with no absolute anchor to correct against if either one drifts. Absolute targets give the model something fixed to regress toward regardless of accumulated error. This is the first data point in a pattern that recurs throughout this post: representations that seem more "natural" or "compact" can be strictly harder to learn from, for reasons that have nothing to do with model capacity.

Cautionary tale #2: redundant dimensions aren't just wasted space — they're a bug waiting to happen

Here's something specific to this robot's kinematics that turned out to matter more than expected. Of the 12 recorded joint dimensions, 5 are exact deterministic functions of the other 7: the four pitch-linkage joints are fixed linear transforms of one reference joint (the wrist mimics a parallelogram), and one gripper joint is exactly the negative of the other (gripper1 = -gripper2). The true information content of a 12-dim joint-space action is 7 dimensions. The rest is a known transform, not new information.

The obvious framing is "wasted model capacity" — some fraction of training loss spent on dimensions that don't need to be learned at all, since they're trivially predictable from the other 7. That's real, but it turned out to be the smaller problem.

The sharper one only became visible while refactoring the control stack this project runs on. The low-level controller used to enforce mimic-consistency as a side effect of a completely unrelated mechanism — it silently overwrote whichever values a caller sent for those 5 redundant joints, recomputing them from the 7 independent ones, every single call, regardless of whether the caller had already supplied its own (possibly slightly wrong) values. Every past evaluation of a learned policy on this robot — which has always sent a full 12-dim prediction to the low-level joint controller — got its own small approximation error on those 5 dimensions silently corrected before it ever reached a physical joint. Nobody designed that safety net on purpose; it fell out of an implementation detail elsewhere in the stack.

When that unrelated mechanism was fixed (it was also the source of a real, separately-diagnosed action-execution bug — more on that in a future post), the silent correction went away with it, and for a moment, a learned policy's small, structurally-expected noise on those 5 redundant dimensions would have been sent straight to the mechanism uncorrected: an asymmetric gripper, a parallelogram linkage that doesn't quite close. Caught and fixed before it reached an actual evaluation run, but it's a clean illustration of the point — redundant dimensions in an action representation aren't a purely aesthetic inefficiency. They're surface area for exactly this kind of latent inconsistency, and the model was never trained to get them exactly right, because its own training targets were always generated under whatever correction happened to be in place at data-collection time.

An action representation that never includes those 5 dimensions in the first place doesn't reduce this risk. It eliminates it by construction.

Why not just use quaternions, then?

If the fix for redundancy is "predict pose, not joint angles," the next question is which pose representation. The obvious choice — position plus quaternion — has a specific, well-documented problem for network-predicted rotations: a unit quaternion lives on a constrained manifold (unit norm), and standard per-dimension normalization (mean/std or min/max, computed independently per channel) doesn't respect that constraint. Normalize each of the four quaternion components separately and the result can drift off the unit sphere; renormalizing after the fact partially undoes whatever the network learned to predict in the first place. Quaternions also have the double-cover property (q and -q represent the same rotation), which can create a multi-modal regression target for a model that has no reason to prefer one sign convention over the other.

The standard fix in the pose-estimation and robot-learning literature (Zhou et al., 2019 — the "continuous 6D rotation representation" paper) is to predict the first two columns of a rotation matrix directly (6 unconstrained numbers) and reconstruct a valid orthonormal rotation via Gram-Schmidt at inference time. No hard constraint to violate during training, no discontinuity, and it composes cleanly with ordinary normalization pipelines. This is a genuinely well-established pattern, not a novel idea — worth naming explicitly here because it's easy to reach for quaternions by default (they're already lying around in most robotics codebases, including this one's own IK/FK utilities) without weighing the regression-target cost against the 6D alternative.

Does the task even need full SE(3)?

One thing worth checking before reaching for a general-purpose rotation representation at all: does the task actually use the full rotational degree of freedom the representation would provide? For peg transfer specifically — the answer is no. The oracle's own grasp geometry keeps the tool pointed straight down at all times; the only rotational degree of freedom that ever varies is yaw, driven by which edge of the block is being grasped. Predicting a full quaternion or 6D rotation for this task means asking the model to represent degrees of freedom the task never exercises — plausibly harder to learn than the constrained problem actually in front of it, for zero benefit. A reduced representation — position plus a single yaw angle plus gripper — may be the more appropriate comparison point for this specific task, with full 6D/SE(3) reserved for tasks that genuinely need arbitrary orientation.

This is worth stating as a general principle, not just a project-specific footnote: the "richest" representation available isn't automatically the right one. The right representation is the smallest one that actually spans the degrees of freedom the task uses.

Why VLA models don't care which one you pick

A separate, reassuring point: this isn't an architecture decision. Modern VLA policies — SmolVLA included — are action-space agnostic at the model level. The action expert predicts a fixed-size vector per chunk step; what that vector means is entirely determined by the dataset's declared action schema. Switching from a 12-dim joint-space action to a 5-to-8-dim pose-based one requires zero changes to the model architecture — the flow-matching/regression head, the chunking mechanism, the n_action_steps horizon, all of it carries over unchanged. What changes is purely upstream: the dataset schema, the normalization statistics, and (for a fair comparison) the low-level control interface the predicted actions get executed through.

That last point mattered enough to build for real: this project's low-level arm controller now exposes the same Cartesian-pose interface its own scripted oracle already uses internally. A policy predicting pose targets doesn't need a separate execution path bolted on — it becomes just another client of the exact interface already trusted to be correct, inheriting the same guarantees (proper inverse-kinematics solving, deterministic linkage-consistency, no drift) the ground-truth demonstrations were generated through in the first place. That symmetry — training data and policy inference sharing one control path — is itself a real design decision, not a given.

Predicting end-effector pose rather than joint angles is also, more broadly, the more common convention in generalist VLA work outside this project (RT-1/RT-2-style and similar systems typically favor task-space or pose-relative actions), largely because it transfers better across differently-shaped robots than joint angles do. A joint-space action for a 6-DOF arm means nothing to a 7-DOF arm; a Cartesian pose target means roughly the same thing to both.

The plan

This project is running the comparison directly rather than assuming an answer:

  1. Joint-space baseline, confirmed first. A full data recollection under the corrected control architecture, retraining ACT on joint-space actions exactly as before — a clean, single-variable confirmation that the control-layer fixes described above (and in earlier posts in this series) actually change measured behavior, before touching the action representation at all.
  2. SE(3)/reduced-pose comparison, next. Same task, same demonstrations (reusable via forward kinematics on the already-collected joint-space data, not necessarily a second collection run from scratch), predicting position + yaw + gripper (or full 6D rotation, if the task turns out to need it) instead of 12 joint angles, executed through the same shared control interface the oracle itself uses.

The point of running joint-space first isn't caution for its own sake — it's that without a clean joint-space number to compare against, any difference measured under SE(3) actions would be confounded with the architecture fix itself, and this project has already been burned once this cycle by not isolating variables carefully enough.
